1\. Section 1.1 Current Situation 现状（标题下方第一块正文）

1.1 Current Situation

More than 30 000 students with disabilities are enrolled at Chinese
universities, and thousands of students with mobility impairments enter
higher‑education campuses every year. Many universities have completed
basic accessibility hardware construction including ramps, accessible
elevators and barrier‑free restrooms. However, physical hardware alone
cannot guarantee continuous, safe and independent movement across
campus. Research data shows unassisted wheelchair users travel at only
0.67 m/s; they struggle to navigate curb ramps when slopes exceed 1/5,
and cannot judge whether a route is passable before arriving on‑site.
National special‑education policy from 2026‑2030 encourages universities
to advance digitalised, inclusive campus accessibility construction and
pushes institutions to turn hardware facilities into usable, real‑life
inclusive experiences.

1.1 现状

全国高校在校残障学生超过30000名，每年有数千名行动障碍学生进入大学校园。许多高校已经完成坡道、无障碍电梯、无障碍卫生间等基础无障碍硬件建设。但仅有物理硬件，并不能保障学生在校园内连续、安全、独立通行。研究数据表明，无辅助的轮椅使用者行进速度仅0.67米每秒；当路缘坡道坡度大于1/5时通行会十分困难，并且他们无法在抵达现场前判断路线是否实际可通行。国家2026‑2030特殊教育相关政策鼓励高校推进数字化、包容性校园无障碍建设，推动院校把硬件设施转化为真实可用的包容体验。

2\. Section 1.2 Three core existing problems
现存三大核心问题（现状板块下方）

1.2 Three core existing problems

\- Static campus maps fail to capture temporary dynamic barriers such as
construction zones, blocked walkways, parked shared‑bikes and sudden
elevator breakdowns. These unrecorded dynamic hazards raise travel risks
and greatly reduce independence for mobility‑impaired students.

\- There is a lack of a unified closed‑loop reporting‑maintenance
workflow. Students cannot easily submit barrier reports and track
whether issues are inspected, repaired or formally resolved by
university departments. Faulty facilities may remain broken for weeks
with no clear progress feedback for affected students.

\- Uneven campus spatial design creates persistent obstacles for
independent mobility. Fragmented accessible routes create spatial
inequality across campus. Many accessibility‑related facilities suffer
the "built‑but‑hard‑to‑use" issue: hardware exists on paper yet cannot
serve students in daily real‑world scenarios.

2.1 Product Overview

CampusAble is a campus‑specific digital accessibility platform built to
resolve real‑world mobility barriers within university grounds. It
follows a dual‑side product architecture: a lightweight student‑facing
interface accessible via both mobile devices and standard web browsers,
paired with a role‑based, secure university‑admin management dashboard.
The platform is intentionally designed for higher‑education contexts
rather than general public navigation. It unifies three core
capabilities: personalised accessibility navigation, crowdsourced
barrier incident reporting, and full‑cycle university maintenance
management. All modules interoperate as one integrated system, closing
the historical gap between student on‑campus experiences and
institutional facility‑management workflows. It does not require
large‑scale hardware renovation; instead it maximises the value of
existing campus physical facilities through digital information
management.

2.2 Core basic features (student‑facing side)

\- Users select their own mobility profile before route calculation; the
system generates personalised accessibility‑adjusted routes instead of
default shortest‑path directions. Mobility profiles represent different
real‑world user conditions such as manual wheelchair users,
crutch‑users, or students with temporary lower‑limb injuries.

\- Real‑time status lookup for key campus accessibility‑related
facilities including ramps, accessible elevators and accessible
restrooms. Each facility entry displays verified availability status, so
users can check conditions before leaving their starting location.

\- Crowdsourced barrier‑reporting function with geotag location marking,
optional photo attachments and short written descriptions for on‑site
campus obstacles. User submissions can be saved as local drafts first,
allowing students to complete and submit reports later when they have
stable network access. After submission, end‑users are able to view
public progress updates for their reported incidents.

2.3 Augmented admin features (university dashboard)

\- Closed‑loop ticket workflow: verify‑assign‑track‑close. Valid
student‑submitted barrier reports auto‑generate traceable digital
maintenance tickets inside the admin portal. Authorised university
administrators are allowed to set multi‑level priority levels according
to barrier severity, update real‑time facility operating status, add
internal handling notes, and formally close completed work orders once
repairs are finished.

\- Proactive alert notification function for pre‑planned campus changes.
Administrators can publish advance warnings for scheduled construction
work, temporary ramp closures or elevator maintenance. These alerts will
be visible for all platform users, shifting part of campus accessibility
communication from reactive complaint‑handling to proactive risk
notification.

\- Export downloadable accessibility‑audit datasets for campus long‑term
accessibility‑improvement planning. Administrators can apply multiple
filters: barrier type, location, time range and resolution status.
Aggregated statistical outputs help institutions analyse recurring
campus‑accessibility pain‑points and provide evidence reference for
campus infrastructure upgrade decision‑making.

2.4 Workflow diagram

Visual closed‑loop workflow logic:

User selects mobility profile and generates routes → User submits
geotagged barrier report (with photos/notes, saved as draft if needed) →
University designated staff verify submitted incident content →
Traceable maintenance ticket is automatically created, priority assigned
→ Maintenance task gets executed → After maintenance completion,
platform updates public map routes and facility status information,
closing the full‑cycle workflow.

4.1 Primary Audience

4.1.1 End‑users: Permanently or temporarily mobility‑impaired
university‑campus students

Group characteristics:

This group includes full‑time undergraduate and postgraduate students
with permanent physical impairments such as wheelchair‑users, crutch or
walking‑aid users. It also covers students experiencing temporary
mobility loss caused by fractures, surgery or sports injuries.

Common features of this user group:

1\. They take part in all normal campus activities including classes,
library study, dining‑hall visits and group events, and need to travel
frequently across different campus zones every day.

2\. They face high travel uncertainty: they cannot tell in advance
whether ramps, lifts and walkways are usable. Bad route choices may
cause them to be late for class or even trap them in certain locations.

3\. They have strong willingness for independent living: most avoid
frequently asking classmates or volunteers for assistance. They wish to
reduce dependence on other people and control their own campus
schedules.

4\. They have real‑world experience of campus barriers: they can spot
broken facilities, blocked passages and design flaws first‑hand, and are
willing to report problems if there exists a simple, reliable submission
channel.

Core user needs:

Reliable pre‑trip route assessment, real‑time facility status, simple
barrier‑reporting function and visible feedback for submitted issues.

4.1.2 Paying customers: University facility‑management departments and
student‑service departments

Group characteristics:

These are internal administrative departments within higher‑education
institutions. They hold statutory obligations for campus accessibility
management.

Common features of this customer group:

1\. They receive scattered accessibility complaints from multiple
separate channels including telephone calls, email, offline feedback
forms and student social‑media posts. Information is fragmented across
different teams.

2\. They are accountable for regulatory compliance audits. Universities
need to produce evidence and statistics to prove they are managing
campus accessibility risks properly.

3\. They want to improve operational efficiency: repeated identical
complaints consume large amounts of administrative manpower. They hope
to locate high‑risk barrier locations from aggregated data.

4\. They require traceable records: they need to track who raised a
problem, who handled it and when the issue gets resolved for internal
work assessment.
